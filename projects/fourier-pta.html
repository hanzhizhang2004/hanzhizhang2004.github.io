Fourier-Domain Quadratic Statistics in Pulsar Timing Arrays

Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics (AEI)  ·  Supervised by Dr. Rutger van Haasteren

See the slides of my AEI talkPDF · 20 slides ↗

1Background

Pulsar timing arrays

A pulsar is a rapidly spinning neutron star that sweeps a beam of radiation past the Earth once per rotation, as regular as a good clock. A pulsar timing array (PTA) is an array of such pulsars spread over the sky. Each one sends a train of pulses to the Earth, and we measure their times of arrival (TOAs). A timing model predicts when each pulse should arrive, and the difference between the observed and the predicted arrival time is called the timing residual.

The residuals have several sources. Errors in measuring each arrival time give white noise, uncorrelated from one observation to the next. The rotation of the pulsar itself wanders slightly, which gives red noise, and the interstellar plasma delays radio pulses by an amount that drifts in time and scales as $\nu_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-2}$ with the observing frequency, the dispersion-measure (DM) variations. Finally, a stochastic background of gravitational waves stretches and squeezes the spacetime between each pulsar and the Earth, and that shifts the arrival times too. Altogether,

$$ T^{\mathrm{obs}} = f(t;\beta) + \underbrace{\delta t_{\mathrm{white}} + \overbrace{\delta t_{\mathrm{rn}} + \delta t_{\mathrm{gw}} + \delta t_{\mathrm{dm}}}^{\text{Fourier}}}_{\delta t}, \tag{1} $$

where $f(t;\beta)$ is the timing model with parameters $\beta$ and $\delta t$ is the residual. The last three processes are slow, and the brace marks them for later: they are the ones we will describe with Fourier modes.

Gamma-ray pulsars

For gamma-ray pulsars we have no reliable residuals at all. The Fermi Large Area Telescope records individual photons, arriving as a Poisson process and far fewer than one per rotation, so there is no clean pulse to time. Instead we work in the Fourier domain, following Valtolina and van Haasteren (2025). The three slow processes are expanded in Fourier modes over the observing span $T$,

$$ \begin{aligned} \delta t_{\mathrm{rn}} + \delta t_{\mathrm{gw}} + \delta t_{\mathrm{dm}} &:= F a \\ &\phantom{:}= \sum_{k}\Big[a_k\cos\!\Big(\frac{2\pi k t}{T}\Big) + b_k\sin\!\Big(\frac{2\pi k t}{T}\Big)\Big]\,\nu_{\mathrm{obs}}^{\alpha}, \end{aligned} \tag{2} $$

with $\alpha = 0$ for the red noise and the gravitational waves, and $\alpha = -2$ for the DM variations. Linearising the timing model about its best-fit parameters $\beta_0$, with design matrix $M$ and offsets $\epsilon$, our timing model becomes

$$ \begin{aligned} T^{\mathrm{obs}} &= f(t;\beta) + \underbrace{\delta t_{\mathrm{white}} + \overbrace{\delta t_{\mathrm{rn}} + \delta t_{\mathrm{gw}} + \delta t_{\mathrm{dm}}}^{\text{Fourier}}}_{\delta t} \\[2pt] &= \underbrace{f(t;\beta_0) + M\epsilon}_{f(t;\beta)} + \delta t_{\mathrm{white}} + F a, \end{aligned} \tag{3} $$

with priors $\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \phi_T \to \infty)$, $\delta t_{\mathrm{white}} \sim \mathcal N(0, W)$ and $a \sim \mathcal N\big(0, \phi(\rho)\big)$, where the hyperparameters $\rho$ set the power spectra. In this picture each pulsar's data, radio or gamma-ray, are compressed into a Gaussian distribution for its Fourier coefficients, with a mean $\hat a$ and a covariance $\Sigma$. Keep these two in mind: they come back in Section 2.

Searching for the background

A gravitational-wave background affects every pulsar at once, so it correlates the residuals of different pulsars. In other words, the covariance of $\delta t$, more precisely of $\delta t_{\mathrm{gw}}$, has off-diagonal terms between pulsars, while every other process stays inside a single pulsar. The natural test is then a null hypothesis $H_0$, in which the covariance $N$ of $\delta t$ is (block) diagonal in the pulsars, against an alternative $H_1$, in which the covariance $C$ is not. In the time domain the two are

$$ \begin{aligned} N &= \big\langle \delta t\,\delta t^{\mathsf T}\big\rangle_{H_0} = \overbrace{\widetilde W}^{W + M\phi_T M^{\mathsf T}} + F\phi_N(\rho)F^{\mathsf T}, \\ C &= \big\langle \delta t\,\delta t^{\mathsf T}\big\rangle_{H_1} = \widetilde W + F\phi_C(\rho)F^{\mathsf T}, \end{aligned} \tag{4} $$

where $\widetilde W$ collects the white noise and the timing-model offsets $M\epsilon$. It is ill-defined as written, because $\phi_T \to \infty$, but its inverse is perfectly fine, and the inverse is all we will ever need. The Fourier prior covariance under $H_1$ is

$$ \phi_C = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \mathrm{RN}_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \mathrm{RN}_P \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \Gamma_{11}\mathrm{GW} & \cdots & \Gamma_{1P}\mathrm{GW} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \Gamma_{P1}\mathrm{GW} & \cdots & \Gamma_{PP}\mathrm{GW} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathrm{diag}\left(S_{\mathrm{rn}}(f_1;\rho),\,\dots,\,S_{\mathrm{rn}}(f_n;\rho)\right)\, +\, \Gamma\otimes S_{\mathrm{gw}}(\rho)} \tag{5} $$

The first matrix holds the red noise of each of the $P$ pulsars, and the second holds the gravitational-wave background, shared between pulsars $a$ and $b$ with weight $\Gamma_{ab}$. Under $H_0$ we keep only the diagonal blocks, $\phi_N = \mathrm{diag}(\phi_C)$: the background is still there in every pulsar, but uncorrelated between them.

The weights $\Gamma_{ab}$ form the Hellings–Downs curve,

$$ \Gamma_{ab} = \frac12\,\delta_{ab} + \frac12 - \frac{x_{ab}}{4} + \frac32\,x_{ab}\ln x_{ab}, \qquad x_{ab} = \frac{1 - \cos\zeta_{ab}}{2}, \tag{6} $$

where $\zeta_{ab}$ is the angle between pulsars $a$ and $b$ as seen from the Earth. It records how strongly the gravitational-wave residuals of two pulsars are correlated, and it depends on nothing but their separation on the sky. This quadrupolar pattern is the fingerprint of a gravitational-wave background, and it is what every PTA search is after.

The optimal statistic

With $N$ and $C$ in hand, van Haasteren (2025) looks for the best detection statistic that is quadratic in the data $z$, and finds

$$ D = z^{\dagger} Q z, \qquad Q = \frac{N^{-1}(C - N)\,N^{-1}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\big(N^{-1}(C - N)\big)^2\Big]}}. \tag{7} $$

This filter is optimal in a precise sense: among all quadratic filters, it maximises $\langle L\rangle_{H_1}$, where the deflection

$$ L(z) := \frac{D(z) - \langle D(z)\rangle_{H_0}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{H_0}\big(D(z)\big)}}. \tag{8} $$

counts how many null standard deviations separate $D$ from its null mean. Since $N$ is the block-diagonal part of $C$, the diagonal blocks of $Q$ vanish, so the statistic listens only to the cross-correlations between pulsars. There is one catch. All of this lives in the time domain, on residuals $\delta t$ that gamma-ray pulsars do not have.

The question

Can the optimal statistic, and the p-value that comes with it, be computed from Fourier coefficients alone?

My task: move the whole hypothesis test into the Fourier domain, so that gamma-ray pulsar timing arrays can use it.

2The Fourier detection statistic

Define the Fourier variables

$$ \Sigma^{-1} = F^{\mathsf T}\widetilde W^{-1}F + \phi_N^{-1}, \qquad \hat a = \Sigma\,F^{\mathsf T}\widetilde W^{-1}\delta t, \tag{9} $$

where the timing model is marginalised through

$$ \widetilde W^{-1} = W^{-1} - W^{-1}M\big(M^{\mathsf T}W^{-1}M\big)^{-1}M^{\mathsf T}W^{-1}. $$

These are precisely the mean and covariance of the Fourier-domain Gaussian from Section 1. Then the time-domain statistic becomes a statistic of $\hat a$ alone,

$$ \begin{gathered} D = \hat a^{\mathsf T} Q_{\mathrm{Fourier}}\, \hat a, \qquad \delta\phi = \phi_C - \phi_N, \\ Q_{\mathrm{Fourier}} = \frac{\phi_N^{-1}\,\delta\phi\,\phi_N^{-1}}{\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\big((\Sigma^{-1} - \phi_N^{-1})\,\delta\phi\,\phi_N^{-1}\Sigma\big)^2\Big]}}. \end{gathered} \tag{10} $$

The factor of 2 is there because $\hat a$ is real, made of cosine and sine coefficients, and for real Gaussian data Isserlis' theorem doubles the variance of a quadratic form compared with the complex data of (7). The numerator is the time-domain filter in disguise. With $C - N = F\,\delta\phi\,F^{\mathsf T}$ and the Woodbury identity, which gives $N^{-1}F = \widetilde W^{-1}F\,\Sigma\,\phi_N^{-1}$, every $\delta t$ in $\delta t^{\mathsf T}N^{-1}(C - N)N^{-1}\delta t$ ends up inside $\hat a$. So the statistic needs only three ingredients, $\phi(\rho)$, $\Sigma$ and $\hat a$, and none of them asks for a single arrival time. For a gamma-ray pulsar, $\Sigma$ and $\hat a$ come from its photons, as in Section 1. The full derivation is in my logbook (pp. 3–5), and on random test data the two statistics agree to machine precision (p. 14 on).

3Dimension reduction

A p-value needs the distribution of $D$ under $H_0$, and that means decomposing a matrix as large as the number of Fourier coefficients in the whole array, which quickly gets slow. Fortunately $Q$ is not full rank. The difference $\delta\phi$ is non-zero only for the $r$ coefficients that carry cross-correlations, the frequencies where we model the background. So we define a projector from the $n$-dimensional space down to those $r$ directions, take a thin SVD of the filter in between, and shrink the quadratic form in the numerator of $D$ step by step without changing its value.

n dims$$ \hat a^{\mathsf T}\,\phi_N^{-1}\,\delta\phi\,\phi_N^{-1}\,\hat a $$
Project $\delta\phi$ onto its support with $P = GG^{\mathsf T}$, so $\delta\phi = G\,\Delta\Phi\,G^{\mathsf T}$ with $\Delta\Phi = G^{\mathsf T}\delta\phi\,G \in \mathbb R^{r\times r}$
n dims$$ \hat a^{\mathsf T} A\,\Delta\Phi\,A^{\mathsf T}\hat a, \qquad A := \phi_N^{-1}G \in \mathbb R^{n\times r} $$
Thin SVD (or QR) of the filter, $A = G_A R$, and insert $G_A G_A^{\mathsf T}$, which leaves $A$ unchanged
n dims$$ \hat a^{\mathsf T} G_A G_A^{\mathsf T} A\,\Delta\Phi\,A^{\mathsf T} G_A G_A^{\mathsf T}\hat a $$
Keep the $r$ coordinates $y := G_A^{\mathsf T}\hat a$
r dims$$ y^{\mathsf T}\,\Phi_N\,\Delta\Phi\,\Phi_N^{\mathsf T}\,y, \qquad \Phi_N := G_A^{\mathsf T}\phi_N^{-1}G \in \mathbb R^{r\times r} $$

Here $G \in \mathbb R^{n\times r}$ simply picks out the cross-correlated coefficients, and $G_A$ has orthonormal columns spanning the range of $A$. Every matrix in the last line is $r\times r$, and the statistic is exactly the one we started with (logbook, pp. 9–11).

4p-value evaluation

The p-value asks how often $H_0$ alone would produce a statistic at least as large as the one we observe. Under $H_0$ the reduced data $y$ are Gaussian, with covariance $G_A^{\mathsf T}(\phi_N - \Sigma)G_A$ (logbook, p. 12), so $D$ is a quadratic form in Gaussian variables: a generalised $\chi^2$ variable. Following the recipe of van Haasteren (2025), the p-value takes three steps, with $\widetilde Q$ the reduced filter $\Phi_N\Delta\Phi\,\Phi_N^{\mathsf T}$ of Section 3, divided by the normalisation of (10).

Step 1 · Whiten$$ \Sigma_y \equiv \big\langle y\,y^{\mathsf T}\big\rangle_{H_0} = G_A^{\mathsf T}(\phi_N - \Sigma)\,G_A = LL^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \eta \equiv L^{-1}y \sim \mathcal N(0, I) $$
The data are now white
Step 2 · Eigen-decomposition$$ D = \eta^{\mathsf T}\big(L^{\mathsf T}\widetilde Q L\big)\,\eta = z^{\mathsf T}\Lambda z = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_i\,z_i^2, \qquad z \sim \mathcal N(0, I) $$
A weighted sum of $\chi^2_1$ variables
Step 3 · Right-tail p-value via Imhof$$ p = \mathbb P\{D \ge D_{\mathrm{obs}} \mid H_0\} = 1 - F_{g\chi^2}\big(D_{\mathrm{obs}};\{\lambda_i\}\big) $$

The hyperparameters $\rho$ are themselves uncertain, so in the end this p-value is averaged over posterior samples of $\rho$, which gives the posterior predictive p-value.

5Data demonstration

I ran the pipeline on the Fourier-domain data of a gamma-ray pulsar timing array: 35 pulsars over 12.5 years, each with 10 frequencies (20 Fourier coefficients), and the background modelled on the lowest 5. The per-pulsar Fourier posteriors and the MCMC samples of the noise and background hyperparameters were provided by Serena Valtolina. The observed statistic sits comfortably inside the null distribution, with $p = 0.348$. Averaging over the posterior samples that ship with the code gives a posterior predictive $p \approx 0.33$. So this gamma-ray array shows no significant Hellings–Downs correlation. Two checks back the numbers up. The reduced statistic agrees with the full 700-dimensional one to twelve digits, and Imhof's distribution matches the Monte Carlo draws ($p = 0.348$ against $0.3476 \pm 0.0008$). The reduction also pays off: the p-value takes 31 ms instead of 133 ms.

Relativistic Mean-Motion Resonance

Tsinghua University  ·  Supervised by Prof. Huan Yang

Since October 2026, as a research assistant with Prof. Huan Yang at Tsinghua University, I have been studying mean-motion resonances between two small bodies orbiting a black hole, within gravitational self-force theory.

1Background

A small body orbiting a much heavier black hole loses energy to gravitational waves and slowly spirals in: an extreme-mass-ratio inspiral (EMRI). Gravitational self-force theory is the most accurate description of this motion. On my self-force project page I explain how it works: the body's stress–energy perturbs the metric, and the perturbation, fed back into the geodesic equation, corrects the orbit.

With two small bodies the metric perturbation has two sources. Body $s$ ($s = 1, 2$) feels its own field only through its regular part, as a single body does, and the field of the other body $b$ in full, since that field is already smooth at body $s$:

$$ u_s^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}u_s^{\mu} = \underbrace{\mathcal A_s^{\mu}\big[h_s^{R}\big]}_{\text{self}} + \underbrace{\mathcal A_s^{\mu}\big[h_b^{\rm ret}\big]}_{\text{mutual}} + O(\epsilon^{2}). \tag{1} $$

Mean-motion resonance. The mutual pull is weak, and usually it averages out: each time the two bodies line up with the black hole they do so at a different point of their orbits, so successive kicks point in different directions. If the orbital frequencies are in an integer ratio, the line-ups recur at the same point, the kicks add up orbit after orbit, and they can lock the bodies together. Neptune and Pluto are held in a 3:2 resonance in this way.

Geodesic motion in Schwarzschild is integrable. For equatorial orbits each body has two actions $\mathbf J_s = (J_s^{r}, J_s^{\phi})$, constant along the geodesic, and two conjugate angles $\mathbf w_s = (w_s^{r}, w_s^{\phi})$, which advance uniformly at the radial and azimuthal frequencies $\boldsymbol\nu_s = \partial E_s/\partial\mathbf J_s$. For a pair of integer vectors $\mathbf N = (\mathbf N_1, \mathbf N_2)$, the resonant angle and its rate are

$$ \begin{aligned} \Psi &= \mathbf N\cdot\mathbf w = \mathbf N_1\cdot\mathbf w_1 + \mathbf N_2\cdot\mathbf w_2, \\ \dot\Psi &= \Delta(\mathbf J) \equiv \mathbf N_1\cdot\boldsymbol\nu_1 + \mathbf N_2\cdot\boldsymbol\nu_2. \end{aligned} \tag{2} $$

The bodies are in resonance on the surface $\Delta(\mathbf J) = 0$, where $\Psi$ stands still; near it $\Psi$ changes slowly, while every other combination of the angles turns at orbital rates. For the 2:1 resonance of Section 4, the value of $\Psi$ fixes where the line-ups happen: at the inner body's periapsis for $\Psi = 0$, at its apoapsis for $\Psi = \pi$ (Figure 1a). Around a black hole $\nu^{r} \neq \nu^{\phi}$ (periapsis precession), so each Newtonian resonance splits into several distinct ones.

The aim of the project is to describe these resonances within self-force theory and to find their imprint on the gravitational waves.

2Equations of motion near resonance

Under the self-force the actions are no longer constant: $\dot{\mathbf J} = O(\epsilon)$ and $\dot{\mathbf w} = \boldsymbol\nu(\mathbf J) + O(\epsilon)$, with $\epsilon \sim \mu/M$. The forcing is periodic in all four angles, a sum of harmonics $e^{i\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf w}$. Most of them turn at orbital rates and average out. Near the resonance the harmonics along $\mathbf N$, $\mathbf n = p\mathbf N$, turn only at the slow rate $p\Delta$ and survive. Removing the fast harmonics by a near-identity change of variables leaves the normal form

$$ \dot\Psi = \Delta(\mathbf J) + O(\epsilon), \qquad \dot{\mathbf J} = \epsilon\Big[\,\mathcal G^{J}_{0} + \sum_{p\neq 0}\mathcal G^{J}_{p\mathbf N}\,e^{ip\Psi}\Big] + O(\epsilon^{2}). \tag{3} $$

The resonance matters only within $|\Delta| \lesssim \epsilon^{1/2}$ of the resonance surface, where it acts on the time scale $M\epsilon^{-1/2}$, between the orbital time $M$ and the inspiral time $M\epsilon^{-1}$. There, differentiating $\dot\Psi = \Delta(\mathbf J)$ gives, up to $O(\epsilon^{3/2})$, a single equation for $\Psi$, with all coefficients evaluated on $\Delta = 0$:

$$ \ddot\Psi = \epsilon\,\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial\mathbf J}\cdot\Big[\underset{\substack{\big\uparrow \\ \llap{\text{zero mode: own self-force}} \\ \llap{\left(\langle\dot{\mathbf J}_1\rangle,\,\langle\dot{\mathbf J}_2\rangle\right)} \\ \llap{\text{purely dissipative}}}}{\mathcal G^{J}_{0}} + \sum_{p\neq 0}\underset{\substack{\big\uparrow \\ \rlap{\text{non-zero modes: mutual interaction}}}}{\mathcal G^{J}_{p\mathbf N}}\,e^{ip\Psi}\Big]_{\Delta=0}, \tag{4} $$ $$ \mathcal G^{J}_{p\mathbf N} = \Big(-ip\,\mathbf N_1\big(\underset{\substack{\uparrow \\ \llap{\text{conservative}}}}{V_p} - i\,\underset{\substack{\uparrow \\ \rlap{\text{dissipative}}}}{u_p}\big),\;\; -ip\,\mathbf N_2(V_p + i\,u_p)\Big), \tag{5} $$

Here $V_p$ and $u_p$ are real. The two kinds of coefficients come from different physics. A body's self-force depends only on its own angles, so it contains no $e^{ip\Psi}$, which involves both bodies; the mutual forcing is the angle derivative of an interaction energy, so it averages to zero. Hence the zero mode is pure self-force and the resonant harmonics are pure mutual interaction. By time-reversal symmetry the conservative part of the self-force averages to zero over the orbit, so the zero mode is purely dissipative: it is the radiation reaction that drives the inspiral. In the resonant harmonics, $V_p$ is conservative and only moves energy from one body to the other, while $u_p$ is dissipative and changes the total energy.

3Reading the coefficients off the radiation

Computing these coefficients directly would mean solving for the metric perturbation near the bodies and subtracting its singular part, a hard numerical problem. Energy gives a way around it. Each body's energy is a function of its actions, so it changes as $\dot E_s = \boldsymbol\nu_s\cdot\dot{\mathbf J}_s$. On the resonance surface both bodies share the frequency $\omega_p \equiv p\,\mathbf N_2\cdot\boldsymbol\nu_2 = -p\,\mathbf N_1\cdot\boldsymbol\nu_1$, and equations (3)–(5) turn into three energy statements:

$$ \begin{alignedat}{2} &\text{self:}\quad && \langle\dot E_s\rangle = \boldsymbol\nu_s\cdot\langle\dot{\mathbf J}_s\rangle, \quad \langle\dot L_s\rangle = \langle\dot J_s^{\phi}\rangle, \\ &\text{mutual, dissipative:}\quad && \dot E_1^{\rm diss} = \dot E_2^{\rm diss} = 2\epsilon\sum_{p\ge 1}\omega_p\,u_p\cos p\Psi, \\ &\text{mutual, conservative:}\quad && \dot E_1^{\rm cons} = -\dot E_2^{\rm cons} = -2\epsilon\sum_{p\ge 1}\omega_p\,V_p\sin p\Psi. \end{alignedat} \tag{6} $$

Energy can be counted far from the bodies, at infinity and at the horizon, where the field is a free gravitational wave. Each statement then becomes a scattering problem of its own (Figure 2):

  • One body alone. The energy and angular momentum it radiates give its drift $\langle\dot{\mathbf J}_s\rangle$.
  • Both bodies. On resonance they radiate in the same mode at the same frequency, so their waves interfere, like two loudspeakers playing one note. The total flux minus each body's own flux leaves a cross term that depends on $\Psi$, and gives $u_p$. $V_p$ does not appear, since it only moves energy between the bodies.
  • Body 1 in body 2's wave. Replace body 2 by the wave it sends towards body 1. The energy body 1 gains from this wave is the energy exchanged between the two bodies, and gives $V_p$.

No step needs the metric perturbation near the bodies or its regularisation: the wave amplitudes far away are enough.

4Example: a 2:1 resonance

Take an eccentric inner orbit (semi-latus rectum $10M$, eccentricity $e = 0.1$) and a circular outer orbit, in the 2:1 resonance $\Psi = w_1^{r} - 2w_1^{\phi} + 2w_2^{\phi}$; the condition $\Delta = 0$ places the outer body at $r = 12.97M$. Keeping the leading harmonic, equation (4) becomes a tilted pendulum,

$$ \theta'' = \kappa - \sin\theta, \qquad \theta = \Psi - \Psi_c, \tag{7} $$

where a prime is a derivative with respect to $\omega_{\rm res}t$, with $\omega_{\rm res}$ the frequency of small oscillations, and the phase offset $\Psi_c$, below $0.004$ here, comes from the dissipative coupling. The tilt $\kappa$ compares the push of the inspiral with the pull of the resonance. An island in which the bodies can lock exists only for $|\kappa| < 1$. Figure 3 shows the solutions for this resonance.

Evaluating the coefficients for this resonance gives three conclusions.

  • The bodies can lock. For $e = 0.1$, $|\kappa| < 0.1$ at every mass ratio, so the island is nearly that of an untilted pendulum.
  • Locking appears as an eccentricity oscillation. Across the island the inner body's radial action changes by 10–30%, and one libration lasts about 70–200 inner orbits for $\mu_2/M = 10^{-5}$.
  • Nearly circular orbits do not lock. The coupling grows with the inner eccentricity while the tilt does not, so $|\kappa| \propto 1/e$; for $\mu_1/\mu_2 = 10$ the island disappears below $e \approx 0.008$.
